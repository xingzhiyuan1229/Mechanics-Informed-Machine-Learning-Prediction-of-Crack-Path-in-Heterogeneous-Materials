## ABSTRACT

Predicting crack paths in brittle porous media with geometric or material discontinuities remains a central challenge in fracture mechanics. In this work, we present a machine learning (ML) framework trained on finite element method (FEM) simulation data to predict crack propagation in a heterogeneous medium containing a random distribution of discontinuities or defects. Our findings underscore the necessity of constructing high-quality training datasets from accurate FEM results to capture crack paths reliably. To support this, we used a special boundary condition termed the 'variable-stiffness boundary condition', built on the so-called surfing boundary condition, that stabilizes crack growth even in densely distributed pores or discontinuities. The framework enables consistent data extraction for model training. This targeted dataset generation approach not only improves the relevance of the training data but also reduces the computational cost of the learning process. The trained ML model demonstrates high accuracy in predicting crack trajectories across various distributions of defects and remains robust even beyond the porosity levels used during training. These results highlight the potential of integrating ML with fracture mechanics to enable efficient and precise crack path prediction in porous media, advancing the design of next-generation composite materials.

## 1. Introduction

Brittle materials, such as ceramics, concrete, bone, and semiconductors, are widely used in structural, biomedical, and electronic applications due to their favorable strength-to-weight ratios, thermal resistance, and biocompatibility [1–13]. However, their intrinsic microstructural heterogeneity, particularly the presence of discontinuities (such as defects, voids, pores, microstructure, etc.), significantly influences crack initiation, propagation, and ultimately failure [8,14,15]. The complex interplay between evolving cracks and pore distributions can result in non-trivial fracture behaviors, such as deflection, branching, or arrest, which challenge predictive modeling across scales [16–20].

The interplay between porous and brittle materials' fracture path, strength, and toughness demands a deeper understanding of crack-discontinuity interaction. At the atomic scale, numerous studies have employed molecular dynamics simulations to elucidate the influence of defect characteristics on the mechanical properties of both amorphous and crystalline constituents within heterogeneous mediums. These studies show that, depending on the structural and crystallographic anisotropy, a propagating crack can get arrested or dodged by discontinuity, affecting the strength and toughness of materials [12,13,21]. At the continuum scale, the phase-field-based approach has correctly modeled crack nucleation and propagation in heterogeneous materials [22–24]. This approach allows for the simulation of complex crack patterns, including crack branching, deflection, merging, and crack arrest, enabling the study of fracture behavior in materials with intricate microstructures. However, the simulation of larger systems with multiple characteristic geometric features remains computationally demanding. Machine learning-based models are important to develop to enable larger systems with higher accuracy.

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a compelling alternative to traditional physics-informed simulations, offering both speed and scalability. By learning patterns from large datasets of simulated or experimental crack paths, ML models can capture fracture behavior without resolving the full-field mechanical equations [25,26]. For instance, Worthington and Chew demonstrated that an artificial neural network trained on micromechanical simulations can accurately predict complex, stochastic crack paths in heterogeneous materials, successfully learning the relationship between void defects and fracture behavior. The success of such data-driven models, however, is critically dependent on the quality and physical basis of the training data. While the physics-based training in the former study yields a powerful predictive tool, a contrasting study shows that a purely geometric approach—which “predicts” a crack’s path based only on its previous shape from a single image—results in a superficial model lacking any generalizable or physical basis.

Furthermore, deep learning architectures, including convolutional neural networks and graph-based models, have been applied to atomic configurations to predict fracture energies, crack branching, and chirality-dependent behavior in 2D materials [27–30]. However, applying such techniques to continuum-level porous media is not yet well established, partly because of the difficulty of encoding physics into data representations and the challenge of generating reliable data. Existing studies encounter limitations in addressing these complexities. Several attempts have been made to employ artificial neural networks (ANN) [31,32] for this purpose; however, the predictive model’s dependence on the specific material poses a significant constraint. Furthermore, the efficacy of such models is hindered by the restricted consideration of a limited number of pores in the analysis [25,33]. A key challenge in this space is generating sufficiently rich and diverse datasets that capture the underlying fracture mechanics while remaining computationally tractable. Moreover, existing studies often limit the density, geometry, shape, and size of discontinuities considered, making generalization difficult.

To address these limitations, this study proposes a mechanics-informed ML framework for predicting crack paths in brittle porous materials using transformer-based neural networks. Our approach integrates physical principles in three ways: (i) constructing training datasets from high-fidelity FEM simulations that model fracture mechanics under Mode I loading, (ii) designing a transformer architecture to capture the spatial and sequential dependencies of crack propagation, and (iii) optimizing the training process to align with physically meaningful crack paths via a mean squared error loss function. Unlike physics-based neural networks that embed governing equations directly into the loss function [34,35], our model relies on mechanics encoded in the training data and architecture, offering computational efficiency and scalability. Specially, the model is trained on datasets derived from finite element simulations under stable crack propagation condition to enable the extraction of localized, physically relevant training samples around the heterogeneities in the physical domain. Consequently, our model is not a black-box predictor: physical insight is injected through dataset construction, crack-tip domain selection, and a training scheme that respects material heterogeneity. The model demonstrates strong predictive capability across a range of defect levels, including conditions not seen during training, highlighting its robustness and ability to generalize. To focus on the effects of microstructural heterogeneity, such as pore distributions, on crack propagation, this study specifically investigates Mode I (tensile) loading, while acknowledging that crack trajectories in brittle heterogeneous materials can also be influenced by other loading conditions, such as Mode II (shear) or mixed-mode [36,37].

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 outlines the computational framework. Section 2.1 describes the dataset generation and model architecture. Section 2.2 details the training process. Results comparing ML and FEM predictions are presented in Section 3, followed by conclusions in Section 4.

## 2. Computational framework

The core of our methodology is an iterative machine learning (ML) framework designed to predict crack propagation in heterogeneous media, as illustrated by an iterative workflow presented in Fig. 1.

This autoregressive scheme forecasts the macroscopic crack trajectory by sequentially predicting the path in localized regions around the crack tip. The cycle begins with the identification of a localized sub-domain that contains the current crack tip and its adjacent heterogeneity. Next, this extracted sub-domain is fed into the pre-trained Transformer model, which predicts the subsequent crack path within this local region. The predicted path is then integrated back into the main domain to update the overall crack trajectory. Finally, the model checks if the crack has reached the domain boundary. If it has not, the cycle repeats, starting from the newly updated crack tip location. This iterative, localized approach allows the model to handle large domains efficiently by focusing computational effort only where it is needed—at the advancing crack front. The current implementation of the model is designed to handle a single propagating crack front. The domain extraction and prediction process focuses on one crack tip at a time, and thus, the model does not account for scenarios involving multiple active crack tips or crack branching.

The reliability of the model depends on the quality of the data used to predict the crack path around the propagating crack-tip which is not straightforward due to its dependence on the type of heterogeneity around the crack-tip. The phase-field approach to fracture mechanics has seen remarkable success over the last few decades for studying crack-nucleation and crack-propagation in heterogeneous medium [24,38]. The phase-field approach to fracture is a regularization of the variational theory proposed by Francfort and Marigo, which itself is an extension of Griffith's theory. For a body occupying a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, the total energy $\epsilon$ of the system is the sum of the stored elastic energy and the energy dissipated to create a crack set $\Gamma$. The sharp-crack energy functional is given by [24]:

$$
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u},\Gamma) = \int_{\Omega \backslash \Gamma}\psi (\epsilon (\mathbf{u}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} + G_{c}\mathcal{H}^{d - 1}(\Gamma) \qquad (1)
$$

where $\mathbf{u}$ is the displacement field, $\Gamma$ is the unknown crack path, $\psi (\epsilon (\mathbf{u}))$ is the elastic strain energy density, $G_{c}$ is the material's fracture toughness (critical energy release rate), and $\mathcal{H}^{d - 1}$ is the $(d - 1)$-dimensional Hausdorff measure (i.e., the length or surface area of the crack). The core idea of the phase-field approach is to approximate the sharp crack topology with a continuous scalar field $d(\mathbf{x})\in [0,1]$, often called the "phase-field" or "damage variable". $d(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ represents the undamaged material, and $d(\mathbf{x}) = 1$ represents a fully cracked material. Values between 0 and 1 represent a damaged state in a diffuse band around the crack. The crack surface energy is approximated using the Ambrosio-Tortorelli functional, which relies on a length-scale parameter $\ell_0$ that controls the width of the diffuse crack region. The fracture energy functional $\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)$ is regularized as follows:

$$
G_{c}\int_{\Gamma}dH^{d - 1}\approx \min_{d}\int_{\Omega}G_{c}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{2\ell_{0}} +\frac{\ell_{0}}{2}|\nabla d|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}. \qquad (2)
$$

This approximation, through $\Gamma$-convergence, ensures that as $\ell_0 \to 0$, the regularized functional converges to the original sharp-crack functional. The total regularized energy functional combines the elastic energy, which is degraded by the damage field, and the regularized fracture energy. The total potential energy of the cracked body is given by $E_{\ell_0}(\mathbf{u},d)$:

$$
E_{\ell_0}(\mathbf{u},d) = \int_{\Omega}g(d)\psi (\epsilon (\mathbf{u}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} + \int_{\Omega}G_c\left(\frac{(1 - d)^2}{2\ell_0} +\frac{\ell_0}{2}|\nabla d|^2\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} \qquad (3)
$$

where the strain energy density $\psi (\epsilon) = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon :\mathbb{C}:\epsilon$ is degraded by a function $g(d)$. A common choice is $g(d) = (1 - d)^2 +k$, where $k\ll 1$ is a small parameter to prevent numerical ill-conditioning in fully broken regions. The stress tensor becomes $\sigma = g(d)\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\epsilon}$. The fracture energy (second term) is the regularized surface energy. Note the term is written for $1 - d$ to represent an "intact" material state, which is equivalent to the formulation in the previous section. The solution $(\mathbf{u},d)$ is found by minimizing the functional in Eq. (3). This leads to a coupled system of two Euler-Lagrange equations, solved simultaneously or using a staggered (alternating minimization) scheme. To ensure that cracks cannot heal, a constraint is imposed on the damage evolution. In a time-dependent setting, this is typically enforced as: $\dot{d}(\mathbf{x},t)\geq 0$. In a quasi-static staggered scheme, this is often implemented by ensuring that the damage at the current step, $d_{n+1}$, is never less than the damage at the previous step, $d_{n}$. This formulation transforms the complex problem of tracking a discrete crack surface into solving a coupled system of partial differential equations on a fixed domain, which is a significant computational advantage.

To stabilize crack-growth the so-called 'surfing boundary condition (SBC)' has been developed with a great success in predicting crack path and toughness in composite materials [23]. To accurately capture this behavior, crack growth must occur steadily at a macroscopic level. While methods like the "surfing boundary condition" achieve this by applying a consistent crack opening displacement, they often require non-trivial modifications to existing FEM software. To enhance compatibility with commercial software, reduced implementation complexity, and development of an alternative strategy, we developed the variable stiffness boundary condition (VSBC), which induces macroscopic steady crack propagation under uniform far-field displacement, as depicted in Fig. 2.

The computational domain is partitioned into the primary heterogeneous domain, $\Omega_{\mathrm{d}}$, and a variable stiffness domain, $\Omega_{\mathrm{vsd}}$, at the loading boundaries. This method obviates the need for fundamental changes to the FEM software architecture and uniform displacement is readily applicable in any commercial software packages and creating a set of strips with different elastic modulus is also feasible via a simple Python script. For brevity, here we outline the key features of VSBC and refer the reader to [39] for a detailed elaboration of the method and its validity and application for homogeneous and heterogeneous domains.

The Young's Modulus in $\Omega_{\mathrm{vsd}}$ is not uniform; it varies spatially according to the function $E_{\mathrm{vsd}} = \left(1 - \frac{x}{L_x}\right)^n a + b$, creating a stiffness gradient along the $x$-axis. For this study, we set the profile parameters to $n=8$, $a=0.4$, and $b=0.005$, while the modulus of the primary domain $\Omega_{\mathrm{d}}$ is 1. When uniform displacement is applied at the remote boundary, this stiffness gradient transforms it into a non-uniform displacement field at the interface with $\Omega_{\mathrm{d}}$. This boundary condition is engineered to control the energy flow into the system, ensuring that the energy release rate at the crack tip remains near the material's critical value, $G_c$ (in our simulations, $\ell_0 = 0.6$ with mesh size $h=0.25$). The resulting mechanical response, quantified by the $J$-integral versus timestep, confirms the efficacy of this approach (Fig. 2 (Right)). Following an initial nonlinear increase during crack nucleation ($a-b$) and a subsequent sharp drop as the crack dynamically jumps ($b-c$), the $J$-integral stabilizes to a constant value of 0.1, matching the prescribed toughness of the homogeneous domain. This plateau ($c-d$) signifies a period of steady, well-defined crack growth, consistent with Rice's formulation where the $J$-integral equals the critical driving force for a semi-infinite crack under uniaxial loading.

### 2.1. Generation of ML datasets

Our methodology employs a physically-motivated feature engineering approach by extracting a smaller, localized Region of Interest (ROI) around the crack tip that dictates the evolution of a crack tip based on the stress state and material properties within a localized region known as the fracture process zone. This strategy focuses the learning task on the essential mechanics of crack-pore interactions, significantly enhancing training efficiency and model performance. The finite element model provides the raw, categorical data (intact material, crack, pore) at different spatial locations, and the process of converting this into the ML Dataset (the matrix of 0s and 1s) is binarization wherein the different components of the finite element model (intact material, crack, pore) are converted into a binary matrix where ‘1’ represents the intact domain and ‘0’ represents a crack or void. This abstraction, while discarding the continuous stress and strain fields, preserves the essential geometric information—the relative spatial configuration of the crack and pores—that dictates path tortuosity.

The generation of each training instance is illustrated in Fig. 3.

A controlled finite element model with a simple, well-defined geometry (an $11 \times 6$ domain with an initial crack and a single pore) is subjected to uniaxial deformation (Fig. 3(a)). The resulting crack propagation provides a high-fidelity input-output pair. To make this information tractable for the ML model, the geometry within the $9 \times 4$ ROI (red dotted line) is converted into a binary feature map (Fig. 3(b)). The dimensions of this $9 \times 4$ ROI were not chosen arbitrarily; they are directly informed by the mechanics of the system, as visualized in Fig. 4. This figure shows the Mode I stress field, $\sigma_{yy}$, in homogeneous and heterogeneous domains under the VSBC loading. The region of high stress concentration (in red) represents the physical area where fracture is driven. The $9 \times 4$ ROI, indicated by the red dashed line, is dimensioned to fully encompass this critical stress concentration zone, thereby capturing the microstructural features that influence the crack’s subsequent path.

A critical aspect of our data generation strategy is the temporal consistency enabled by the VSBC. As shown by comparing Figs. 3(a1, a2) and 4(c2, d2), the crack propagates a stable and constant length of 4 units per timestep under a constant applied strain rate. We deliberately set the width of our ROI to match this propagation length. This synchronization is crucial: it ensures that at each step of the autoregressive prediction, the input subdomain seen by the ML model corresponds precisely to the region of heterogeneity that determined the ground-truth crack advancement in the FEM simulation. This creates a stationary learning problem, where the relationship between the input state and the output action remains consistent over time, greatly enhancing the fidelity and stability of the trained model.

### 2.2. Training the artificial neural network (ANN) model

#### 2.2.1. Dataset construction and sampling strategy

The foundation of our supervised learning approach is a comprehensive dataset derived from high-fidelity FEM simulations. To systematically explore the influence of local heterogeneity, we generated a combinatorial set of training examples based on pore configurations within the $9 \times 4$ ROI. This ROI contains 35 discrete locations available for pore placement. Our sampling strategy was designed to encompass scenarios with zero, one, or two pores, as illustrated in Fig. 5(a). This approach included one case with no pores, 35 cases with a single pore at each possible location, and all 595 unique combinations for two pores $(C(35,2))$. In total, this systematic sampling yielded 631 unique FEM simulations. As each simulation provides both an input (initial state) and an output (propagated state) matrix, the final training dataset comprised 1262 numerical matrices, ensuring the model was exposed to a rich and representative basis of crack-pore interactions.

After the FE simulations, the resulting FE models were converted into ML datasets. Each dataset comprises two $9 \times 4$ matrices representing the input and output domains of the FE model. These matrices capture the simulation details within the spatial boundaries outlined by the red dotted line in the computational domain. With a total of 631 cases in the FE model, the ML datasets encompass a collection of 1262 numerical matrices.

#### 2.2.2. Transformer architecture: A physics-motivated choice

For this prediction task, we selected a Transformer-based neural network. While originally developed for natural language processing, the core mechanism of the Transformer—self-attention—is exceptionally well-suited to the physics of fracture in heterogeneous media. The stress field at a crack tip is non-locally influenced by the entire distribution of surrounding pores, not merely the closest one. The self-attention mechanism models this phenomenon directly by computing a weighted score for all elements in the input sequence, allowing the model to learn the complex, long-range dependencies between the crack tip and multiple, arbitrarily placed pores. This provides a theoretical advantage over architectures like CNNs, which are inherently biased towards local correlations, or RNNs, which may struggle to capture complex spatial layouts. The model architecture, implemented in TensorFlow/Keras with a fixed random seed of 42 for reproducibility, processes the input data through a series of specialized layers. An input matrix is first flattened into a 36-dimensional vector representing the material state. This vector is then processed by the core of the model, a multi-head attention layer with eight attention heads (`num_heads = 8`) and a key dimension of 32 (`key_dim = 32`), which captures different subsets of spatial relationships simultaneously. The output from this layer is then stabilized through layer normalization before being passed to a dense feed-forward network with 512 units (`Dense = 512`) and ReLU activation. A dropout layer with a rate of 0.1 is applied subsequently to mitigate overfitting. Finally, a linear output layer with 36 units generates the predicted material state, which is reshaped into the final $9 \times 4$ output matrix. The model is optimized using the Adam optimizer and a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function, which penalizes geometric deviations from the physically accurate FEM trajectories.

$$
L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j = 1}^{N}(y_{j} - \hat{y}_{j})^{2}
$$

Unless otherwise stated, we randomly split the ROI dataset into $(80\%)$ training and $(20\%)$ held-out validation data. The split is performed at the level of full FE simulations: all ROIs generated along the crack path from a given simulation are treated as a single unit and assigned either to the training set or to the validation set, but never to both. Each FEM simulation corresponds to a unique pore configuration within the $(9 \times 4)$ ROI (with zero, one, or two pores), so this simulation-level partitioning prevents near-duplicate ROIs arising from the same underlying pore arrangement and crack trajectory from appearing in multiple subsets. Consequently, the combinatorial construction of the dataset does not introduce data leakage between training and validation.

#### 2.2.3. Hyperparameter optimization and model validation

The model’s performance was rigorously optimized by systematically tuning key hyperparameters, as illustrated in Fig. 5(b–e). The analysis began with the attention mechanism’s complexity, where we found that performance was largely insensitive to increasing the number of attention heads beyond four or the key dimension beyond 16. This suggests that the model’s expressive capacity was not a limiting factor and that the spatial dependencies within the ROI were adequately captured. Next, the training duration was examined. The learning curve in Fig. 5(d) shows validation accuracy saturating around 400 epochs and declining after 900 epochs, a clear sign of overfitting. Concurrently, the network’s capacity, controlled by the ‘Dense’ layer size, was tuned. As shown in Fig. 5(e), increasing the layer size to 512 units improved performance, while further increases led to overfitting. Based on this analysis, the final optimized hyperparameter set was chosen as: `num_heads = 8`, `key_dim = 32`, `Dense = 512`, and `epochs = 500`. Given the significant class imbalance where intact material constitutes $\sim 92\%$ of the pixels, standard accuracy may be a misleading metric. We therefore adopt the F1 score and Intersection over Union (IoU), which are robust to class imbalance and standard in segmentation tasks. The F1 score is approximately 0.9948, and the IoU is approximately 0.9897 (Table 1). In all cases, the F1 score is computed within the near-tip ROI as a binary segmentation problem, where crack and pore pixels are treated as the positive class and intact material as the negative class. For each $9 \times 4$ ROI, the network output is thresholded to obtain a binary crack mask, from which the confusion matrix $\{TP, FP, TN, FN\}$ is constructed based on crack/pore versus non-crack/non-pore pixels. The F1 score is defined in the standard form,

$$
F_{1} = \frac{2TP}{2TP + FP + FN},

## 3. Results & discussion

### 3.1. Predictive accuracy and generalization

In this section, we evaluate the performance of the trained Transformer model by deploying it to predict crack paths in large-scale porous domains and comparing the outcomes against high-fidelity FEM benchmarks. We designed six test cases using a $200 \times 80$ domain with an initial crack of length 20, featuring systematically increasing porosity levels of $0\%$, $1\%$, $2\%$, $3\%$, $4\%$, and $5\%$. To ensure a consistent initiation phase, pores were randomly distributed at a minimum distance of 30 units from the initial crack tips. We also clarify the distinction between validation and test-time evaluations. Tables 1 and 2 report ROI-level metrics on the held-out validation data, where each $(9 \times 4)$ ROI is used to assess single-step, local crack-segmentation performance. In contrast, the “test-time” results presented in the subsequent tables and figures are obtained on separate $(200 \times 80)$ porous domains that are never used for training or hyper-parameter tuning, and instead assess the model’s ability to roll out full crack paths under physically realistic loading conditions. As a result, although the training set systematically enumerates local pore configurations, the model is always evaluated on globally distinct microstructures and crack sequences, ensuring that the reported performance reflects genuine generalization rather than memorization of repeated patterns. The results, visualized in Fig. 6, demonstrate the model’s capacity to capture the complex physics of crack-pore interactions.

In the homogeneous case $(0\%$ porosity), the model accurately predicts a straight, linear-elastic fracture path. As porosity is introduced and increased, the crack trajectories become progressively more complex due to enhanced crack-pore interactions and local stress perturbations. To quantitatively assess this increasing geometric complexity, two complementary metrics—tortuosity and mean deviation from the centerline—were evaluated as functions of porosity. Tortuosity $(\tau)$ is defined as $\tau = \frac{L_{\mathrm{actual}}}{L_{\mathrm{straight}}}$, where $L_{\mathrm{actual}}$ is the measured length of the crack path and $L_{\mathrm{straight}}$ is the straight-line distance between the crack’s start and end points. The deviation from the centerline $(D)$ is defined as $D = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i = 1}^{n} |y_{i} - y_{\mathrm{ref}}|$, where $y_{i}$ denotes the $y$-coordinate of the $i^{\mathrm{th}}$ crack path pixel, $y_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the reference centerline coordinate, and $n$ is the total number of pixels along the crack path. These metrics provide quantitative measures of path irregularity: tortuosity reflects the overall elongation of the crack path relative to a straight trajectory, while mean deviation from the centerline characterizes lateral fluctuations induced by pore interactions.

As shown in Fig. 7, both metrics exhibit an increase with porosity, indicating that higher pore densities promote greater crack deflection and nonlinear propagation behavior. The tortuosity rises from nearly unity in the homogeneous case to $\tau \approx 1.14$ at $5\%$ porosity, reflecting progressively longer and more circuitous crack trajectories. Similarly, the mean deviation from the horizontal centerline increases from 0 to $D \approx 3.3$ pixels, confirming enhanced lateral wandering of the crack path under elevated pore concentrations. This observed tortuosity, a primary mechanism of fracture toughening in porous media, aligns with theoretical models and experimental observations where pores force the crack front to follow a more energy-intensive path [40]. To quantitatively assess the model’s performance and investigate the potential for error accumulation inherent in our iterative, autoregressive framework, we employed the Intersection over Union (IoU) metric. This metric evaluates the spatial overlap between the predicted and ground-truth crack paths at each timestep, providing a stringent measure of accuracy. Fig. 8(a) plots the IoU evolution over 60 timesteps for all porosity cases. All scenarios exhibit an initial IoU near 1.0, indicating high accuracy at the early stages of propagation. As the simulation progresses, a gradual decay in IoU is observed, which is characteristic of autoregressive “roll-out” predictions where minor single-step errors can compound over long sequences [41]. Notably, the rate of decay correlates strongly with microstructural complexity; the $0–2\%$ porosity cases, which fall within the model’s direct training experience, maintain robust stability with an IoU consistently above 0.9. For higher porosities ($3-5\%$), the decline is more pronounced, reflecting the increased challenge of predicting paths through denser, more complex pore fields.

Despite this, the model demonstrates remarkable robustness. As shown in Fig. 8(b), the mean IoU remains above 0.85 for all tested porosities. This level of performance surpasses that of many machine learning models for crack path prediction, which have reported IoU values in the range of 0.57 to 0.74 [42,43]. This provides compelling evidence of the efficacy of our physics-informed training strategy.

The model’s true power lies in its ability to generalize, a capability we probed by analyzing its performance in regions where the local heterogeneity exceeds that of the training data. Fig. 9 examines the 13th prediction step for the $5\%$ porosity case. Here, the extracted ROI (Fig. 9(b1)) contains three pores and has a local porosity of $8.3\%$—a configuration that is explicitly outside the training dataset, which was limited to a maximum of two pores and a local porosity of $5.5\%$. The model successfully predicts the correct path by performing a form of compositional generalization, a key goal in artificial intelligence where a model learns to understand and create novel combinations of familiar concepts [44]. Instead of failing, the Transformer’s self-attention mechanism appears to have learned the “grammar” of crack-pore interactions. It correctly identifies that distant pores (e.g., pore 3) have a negligible influence on the local stress field, a behavior learned from training examples like those in Fig. 9(c3, c5, c6). More significantly, it recognizes that the combined influence of the proximal pores (1 and 2) is a non-linear interaction that is best represented by the two-pore training cases (e.g., Fig. 9(c4)), rather than a simple linear superposition of their individual effects (Fig. 9(c1 and c2)). The model assigns higher attention scores to these more representative combined patterns, allowing it to correctly navigate a novel microstructural arrangement by composing knowledge from simpler, learned patterns.

### 3.2. Baselines & task non-triviality

To demonstrate that crack-path prediction is non-trivial and to motivate the need for our transformer architecture, we benchmark three progressively stronger baselines and all baselines are evaluated against our transformer using the same autoregressive roll-out and ROI inputs. (i) Null (Straight-Line) model: the crack tip advances horizontally across the $9 \times 4$ ROI with zero steering, representing the simplest Mode-I hypothesis. (ii) Rule-based Pore-Attraction model: if any pore lies within the ROI, the tip steers toward the nearest pore, connects to it, and continues from that location; if no pore is present, it propagates straight. Movement is restricted to forward steps only (no backtracking). (iii) CNN baseline: a lightweight convolutional network trained with the same data partitions, inputs/outputs, loss, and roll-out procedure as the transformer. The crack path comparisons are shown in Fig. 10.

The FE reference crack path (Fig. 10(a)) exhibits a gentle downward drift with localized kinks induced by pore-crack interactions. The Null (straight-line) baseline fails to deflect (Fig. 10(b)), whereas the rule-based Pore-Attraction model overreacts to nearby pores and produces zig-zag segments (Fig. 10(c)). The CNN baseline captures the overall trend but smooths out small kinks and accumulates lateral drift (Fig. 10(d)). In contrast, the Transformer closely tracks the FE trajectory, reproducing subtle deflections while maintaining directional stability (Fig. 10(e)). These observations are corroborated quantitatively by the IoU statistics in Fig. 11(a).

The Null and Pore-Attraction baselines deteriorate rapidly once the crack tip enters the pore field (11–12 steps), with IoU dropping below 0.5 by 19–24 steps and settling near 0.12–0.17 at the horizon. The CNN decays more gradually and ends around 0.33. In contrast, the Transformer maintains high overlap throughout, remaining $\approx 0.85$ at mid-trajectory and $\approx 0.76$ at the final steps, indicating markedly reduced error compounding. Summary statistics corroborate these trends (Fig. 11(b)): mean IoU is $\approx 0.43$ (Null), $\approx 0.42$ (Pore-Attraction), $\approx 0.68$ (CNN), and $\approx 0.89$ (Transformer); minimum IoU is $\approx 0.12$, $0.16$, $0.32$, and $0.75$, respectively. Relative to the CNN, the Transformer improves mean IoU by $\approx 31\%$ ($0.89$ vs. $0.68$) and more than doubles the minimum IoU $(\sim 2.3\times; 0.75$ vs. $0.32$). Against the simple baselines, it approximately doubles mean IoU and increases minimum IoU by $\sim 5–6\times$. These results demonstrate that crack-path prediction is non-trivial under identical roll-out and ROI inputs, and that the Transformer delivers superior average accuracy and worst-case robustness.

### 3.3. Discontinuity size effect

In previous studies, we explored the effects of porosity on crack propagation in brittle porous materials. In our binary matrix representation, each binary ‘0’ was modeled as a single-pixel defect, corresponding to a $1 \times 1$ pore. Our training datasets, as shown in Fig. 5(a), utilized $1 \times 1$ pores with configurations limited to zero, one, or two pores. However, another critical aspect remains: the size of individual discontinuities. The discontinuity size effect refers to how pore dimensions influence the local stress fields around a propagating crack. Real-world heterogeneous materials often exhibit pores of varying sizes, which can significantly alter crack-pore interactions, thereby affecting the crack path, tortuosity, and the material’s overall mechanical properties [45,46].

A key question in this research is whether a model trained on $1 \times 1$ pore data can generalize to predict crack propagation in cases with larger pore sizes. Although the model may capture basic trends from $1 \times 1$ pore data, larger pores introduce complex spatial influences and non-linear interactions that may not be adequately represented. This inquiry is critical for evaluating the model’s generalization capabilities and the importance of training data diversity. To investigate this discontinuity size effect and model generalization, we examine square pore clusters with side lengths of 2, 3, and 4 elements, referred to as $2 \times 2$, $3 \times 3$, and $4 \times 4$ pores, respectively, all maintaining a consistent porosity of $5\%$.

Fig. 12(a–b) compares FE and ML predicted crack paths in a porous medium characterized by larger $2 \times 2$ pores with $5\%$ porosity. The ML predictions, shown in yellow, align reasonably well with the FE results depicted in red, capturing the key features and trajectory variations introduced by pore configurations. It is essential to note that the crack paths remain highly dependent on the spatial distribution and local arrangement of the pores, underscoring the significant influence that microstructural discontinuities have on fracture behavior. To quantify the impact of pore size on predictive accuracy, the IoU metric was evaluated. Compared to the baseline scenario of $1 \times 1$ pores, Fig. 12(c) illustrates that the IoU values for larger pores decrease over successive timesteps, indicating that errors may accumulate more rapidly in configurations involving larger discontinuities. Nonetheless, as illustrated in Fig. 12(d), despite this observed decrease, both mean (0.86) and minimum (0.62) IoU values for the $2 \times 2$ pores remain within a reasonable and acceptable range. This finding demonstrates the robustness and generalization capability of the ML model, even though it was originally trained solely on $1 \times 1$ pore data. These results underscore the necessity of incorporating a variety of discontinuity sizes into future training datasets to further enhance model accuracy and applicability to more diverse real-world microstructures.

We have validated our ML model’s predictive accuracy using FE simulations for configurations involving $1 \times 1$ and $2 \times 2$ pores. Building on this established reliability, we now utilize our trained ML model to predict crack propagation in larger pore sizes, specifically $3 \times 3$ and $4 \times 4$ pores, without additional FE validations, as illustrated in Fig. 13. The ML predictions for these larger discontinuities demonstrate that the crack paths accurately capture the increased complexity and trajectory deviations associated with larger pores. Analyzing the crack paths reveals that the crack paths become progressively intricate as the pore size increases, which is consistent with physical expectations and prior FE validated behaviors observed for smaller pores. Given the prior validation for $2 \times 2$ pores, our current ML model can be confidently employed to predict discontinuities of varying sizes. This capability significantly enhances efficiency by circumventing the computationally intensive FE simulations typically required for each new pore configuration. This highlights the model’s robustness and potential for rapid fracture analysis in diverse porous media scenarios.

### 3.4. Probing the limits of extrapolation and the role of dataset richness

To understand the model’s limitations, we challenged it with a significantly more complex environment: a domain with $10\%$ global porosity. The initial model, trained on 631 cases, struggled significantly in this out-of-distribution scenario (Fig. 14(a1)). This is a classic example of out-of-distribution (OOD) failure, where a model trained on one data distribution is unable to generalize to a test case with novel features, a well-documented challenge in deploying machine learning models for scientific applications [47,48]. The predicted path deviates substantially from the FEM ground truth, particularly when encountering dense pore clusters. The reason for this failure becomes clear upon examining the local ROI at the point of divergence (Fig. 14(b1)). The model is faced with a region containing six pores and a local porosity of $17\%$. Furthermore, pores 2, 3, and 4 form a complex, elongated cluster, a feature never seen during training. The model attempts to decompose this novel feature into the simpler two-pore patterns it knows, but this strategy fails because the intricate, non-linear interactions between the closely packed pores are lost.

This failure highlights a critical insight: the model’s predictive power is bounded by the richness of the microstructural patterns in its training data. To address this, we expanded the dataset to 7176 cases by including all three-pore combinations, increasing the maximum experienced porosity to $8.3\%$. After retraining on this enriched dataset, the model’s performance on the same $10\%$ porosity domain improved dramatically (Fig. 14(a2)). The new model now correctly navigates the complex pore cluster because its training vocabulary includes three-pore configurations (Fig. 14(c5)). It no longer needs to rely on a faulty decomposition, treating the cluster as a cohesive entity and predicting its influence accurately.

Fig. 15 provides a quantitative comparison of the two models on the $10\%$ porosity domain. The model trained on the larger dataset maintains a significantly higher IoU over the full 60-timestep rollout, with its final IoU ($\sim 0.46$) being over four times higher than that of the original model ($\sim 0.11$). This demonstrates that expanding the training set to cover more complex local patterns directly enhances the model’s ability to extrapolate beyond its initial training distribution.

## 4. Conclusions

We have developed a mechanics-informed machine learning framework for predicting crack paths in brittle porous media. The framework integrates physical principles through high-fidelity FEM training data, a Transformer architecture that captures long-range spatial dependencies, and an autoregressive rollout strategy that respects local crack-tip physics. Key contributions and findings are summarized as follows:

- **Stable data generation via VSBC**: The variable stiffness boundary condition enables steady crack propagation even in densely distributed pores, providing temporally consistent input-output pairs for ML training.
- **Localized ROI learning**: By focusing on a $9 \times 4$ region around the crack tip, the model learns crack-pore interactions within the fracture process zone, reducing computational cost and improving generalization.
- **High accuracy and robustness**: The Transformer achieves near-perfect F1 scores (0.9948) and IoU (0.9897) on validation data, and maintains mean IoU above 0.85 for porosity levels up to $5\%$, outperforming CNN and rule-based baselines.
- **Generalization beyond training distribution**: The model correctly predicts crack paths for pore sizes up to $4 \times 4$ (trained only on $1 \times 1$ pores) and for local porosities up to $8.3\%$ after enriched training, demonstrating compositional generalization.
- **Limitations and dataset richness**: Extrapolation to $10\%$ global porosity fails with limited training data but succeeds when the training set includes three-pore configurations, underscoring the importance of dataset diversity.

Future work will extend the framework to mixed-mode loading, multiple crack tips, branching, and three-dimensional microstructures. The integration of physics-based loss functions and active learning strategies may further enhance predictive capabilities while reducing data requirements.
